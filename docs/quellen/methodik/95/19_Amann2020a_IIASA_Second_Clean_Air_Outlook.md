Abstract


To support the development of the Second Clean Air Outlook of the European Union, this report presents
a set of model calculations that explore the likely future development of air quality in Europe. The analysis
incorporates recent methodological updates as well as the latest information on emission inventories,
and projections and pollution control policies that have been reported by the Member States in their
official inventory submissions and the National Air Pollution Control Programs.
The resulting emission projections are compared with the obligations of the National Emission reduction
Commitments (NEC) Directive, and their impacts on future air quality are assessed. The report also
examines how additional clean air and climate policies could alter the results of the baseline projection.
For each Member State, the report quantifies the contributions of other countries to PM2.5 exposure
domestically, as well as the destinations of exported pollution. It is found that internationally coordinated
policy action contributed a major share to air quality improvements in the past, and implementation of
the NEC Directive will lead to further effective improvements in European air quality.
The model analysis conducted for this report clearly reveals net benefits from additional actions towards
cleaner air, with positive macro-economic implications.

1 Introduction
The 2013 Clean Air Programme proposed a regular report on the air quality situation in Europe1, also
covering the prospects for future emission reduction and progress towards the EU's objectives. The
analytical basis for such a prospective exercise follows the work done in support to the preparation of
Directive (EU) 2016/2284 on the reduction of national emissions of certain atmospheric pollutants2 – the
National Emission reduction Commitment Directive (NEC Directive). Along these lines, extensive model
analysis (Amann, 2018) has supported the first Clean Air Outlook (COM(2018)446)3 in 2018.
This report summarizes work that has been conducted to inform the development of the Second Clean
Air Outlook in 2020, addressing the following objectives:
      1. Update the analytical framework for the analysis of policy scenarios,
      2. incorporate latest data on emission inventories,
      3. update the model databases to introduce policy developments since the adoption of the first
         Clean Air Outlook, and to include new information provided by Member States in the National
         Air Pollution Control Programmes (NAPCPs) and the National Energy and Climate Plans (NECPs),
      4. develop policy scenarios to quantify:
             the progress towards compliance with the air pollution reduction commitments under the
              NEC Directive and towards the achievement of EU environment and health objectives as set
              out in the 7th Environmental Action Program,
             the related socio-economic impacts, and
             further EU-level measures that would be needed to meet those objectives, and their expected
               impacts.
The main part of this report summarizes the key results of the project, while methodological details and
comparisons with national statistics are provided in the Annex. In this main report, Section 2 describes
the policy scenarios whose results are then analysed in the remainder of the report. Section 3.1 discusses
how the alternative policy assumptions affect emissions of the key air pollutants, and in particular the
achievement of the emission reduction requirements of the NEC Directive. Section 3.2 assesses the
impacts of the emission changes on air quality, addressing the implications for compliance with the air
quality limit values set in the Ambient Air Quality Directives 4 for PM2.5 and NO2 as well as for the
achievement of the WHO air quality guideline value for PM2.5. Consequences for health impact indicators
are summarised in Section 3.3, and for ecosystems impacts in Section 3.4. Section 3.5 presents estimates
of the monetised benefits of health, ecosystems and crop impacts, and provides a full macro-economic
assessment of the costs and benefits of the scenarios. While the main policy scenarios of this report have
been developed for the state of climate policies in 2019, implications of the latest climate policy proposal
from the European Commission in the context of the EU Green Deal are discussed in Section 3.6. The
importance of international cooperation for achieving clean air, and the benefits from EU-wide

2 Policy scenarios considered in this report
To explore the prospects for future emissions and air quality in the EU, this report explores seven policy
scenarios that are based on different combinations of activity projections for different climate policy
choices and air pollution control legislation (Table 2.1).

2.1 Activity projections
2.1.1 The CAO2 baseline projection
The CAO2 baseline activity projection employs for the period up to 2030 the ‘EUCO 32 32.5’ scenario
developed with the PRIMES model to support the Commission’s June 2019 assessment of the draft
national energy and climate plans (NECPs) of the Member States. While it maintains the assumptions on
macro-economic development and fossil fuels prices of the Reference scenario 2016 that has been
employed for the first Clean Air Outlook, the scenario assumes achievement of the EU energy efficiency
target of 32.5% and a renewable energy target of 32% as agreed in the ‘Clean energy for all Europeans’
package until 2030, and implementation of the current policies on non-CO 2 greenhouse gas emissions5.
Beyond 2030, the CAO2 baseline follows the PRIMES baseline projections that extend up to 2050. For the
purposes of air quality assessment and in order to align with the energy statistics that have been used by
Member States for their emission inventories, additional structural detail has been added to the original
PRIMES projections.
To explore the potential implications of additional climate policies, two decarbonisation scenarios of the
EU energy and agricultural systems in line with a stabilisation of global temperature increase at 1.5°C are
analysed.
2.1.2 The 1.5 TECH scenario
The 1.5 TECH scenario explores technological options, including electrification of all end-uses, of
hydrogen as an energy carrier, emergence of carbon-neutral hydrocarbons and efficiency savings, in
order to achieve net zero GHG emissions by 2050. Remaining emissions that cannot be abated by 2050
are balanced by negative emissions through the deployment of biomass associated with carbon capture
and storage (BECCS) and LULUCF sinks.
2.1.3 The 1.5 LIFE scenario
In contrast, the 1.5 LIFE scenario explores the transformation of EU business and consumption patterns
towards a more circular economy. Increased climate awareness of EU citizens translates into lifestyle
changes and consumer choices that are more beneficial for climate. These include a continued trend of
EU consumers towards less carbon intensive diets, a sharing economy in transport, limiting growth in air
transport demand and a more rational use of energy demand for heating and cooling. While both climate
policy scenarios include additional incentives to enhance LULUCF sinks, such incentives are much larger
in the 1.5 LIFE scenario.
For the development of the agricultural sector in the 1.5 LIFE scenario, dietary shifts have been assumed
that reduce total animal calorie intake levels to the recommended levels while keeping the total calorie
consumption constant. The declining consumption trends of bovine, sheep and goat meat and milk
observed in the EU in the last decade are assumed to further continue. Consumption of pig and poultry
meat as well as eggs would remain at the level of 2010.

2.2 Air pollution control policies and measures
Air pollution control policies and measures that are adopted in the various Member States constitute the
second important component of the policy scenarios presented in this report. The following sets of
policies and measures have been compiled
2.2.1 The CAO2 baseline emission control legislation
This package includes the EU legislation on air pollution controls as well as already implemented national
policies and measures. For each of these regulations, the GAINS database holds for each affected source
category in each Member State the temporal penetration, i.e., the evolution over time of the shares of
the emission sources that are subject to the specific regulation.
2.2.2 Additional national policies and measures
This package includes additional national policies and measures that have been reported by Member
States in their NAPCPs and PaMs databases as ‘selected for adoption’ (see Annex 2.2.2), provided they
were described with sufficient level of detail, in addition to the policies and measures of the CAO2
baseline legislation.
2.2.3 Maximum technically feasible emission reductions
The third variant outlines the scope of emission reductions that could be achieved through full application
of all technical emission control measures, as represented in the GAINS database. While excluding
premature scrapping of existing capital stock, this scenario assumes that all new (expansion and
replacement) investments would employ the most efficient emission control measures that are currently
available, subject to constraints emerging from country-specific conditions (e.g., structural composition
of emission sources, vintage distribution, climatic and topographic conditions, firm and farm size
distributions, etc.). As a conservative assumption, no further technological progress is assumed in this
scenario.

2.3 Policy scenarios
This report examines future air quality for seven alternative combinations of activity projections and
emission control packages (Table 2.1).
As a benchmark, the CAO2 baseline combines the CAO2 baseline activity projection with the baseline air
pollution control legislation, which includes already adopted EU-wide and national legislation. The NAPCP
scenario explores the impacts of the additional policies and measures that are reported in the NAPCPs as
‘selected for adoption’. Potential side effects of tightened climate policy measures on air quality are
explored through three climate policy scenarios, i.e., the 1.5 TECH scenario, the 1.5 LIFE scenario, and
the recent Mix55 scenario that has been used in the impact assessment supporting the Commission
proposal for reducing GHG by 55% by 2030 6 . These climate scenarios are combined with clean air
scenarios representing respectively the NAPCP air pollution control measures and the additional
improvements that could be achieved through the maximum feasible application of all available technical
air pollutant emission control measures.

3 Scenario results
For each of the seven policy scenarios listed in Table 2.1, emissions, emission control costs, concentration
maps of the relevant air pollutants, as well as indicators for health and ecosystems impacts have been
calculated, with results reported for each Member State and key economic sector.

3.1 Compliance with the NEC Directive
Table 3.1 to Table 3.5 compare, for each Member State, the emission reduction commitments of the NEC
Directive for 2020-29 and 2030 onwards with the emission projections of the baseline scenario,
considering the most recent updates to the emission inventories for 2005.
For 2020, the CAO2 baseline projection suggests for several Member States higher emissions than what
is required in the NEC Directive. The baseline projections for SO2 do not meet the NEC Directive
requirements for one country, for NOx for four countries, for NH3 for 10 countries, and for NMVOC for
two Member States. However, it has to be noted that the activity statistics that are used in the CAO2
baseline projection for 2020 do not reflect actual statistics as this report has been developed before the
end of 2020. Thus, they do not consider any impacts of the COVID-19 crisis. Instead, they are derived
from projections for 2020 that have been developed in 2018 for the EUCO 32 32.5 scenario.
For 2030, the CAO2 baselines for the energy-related pollutants SO2, NOx, PM2.5 and NMVOC achieve the
emission reduction commitments (ERC) of the NEC Directive for most Member States. For SO2, emissions
are slightly higher in Slovenia; for NOx, higher emissions are computed for the Czech Republic and
Luxembourg, and for PM2.5 for Germany and the Netherlands. For NMVOC, the CAO2 baseline would
require further measures in Ireland and Luxembourg.
In contrast, there are major gaps between the NH3 emissions of the CAO2 baseline and the 2030 ERCs in
22 out of the 27 Member States. For seven Member States the additional policies and measures that
have been reported in the NAPCPs as ‘selected for adoption’ would align NH3 emissions with the ERCs.
This would however mean that 15 Member States would not comply with their NEC Directive obligations.

3.2 Air quality management zones: Background concentrations, EU Air Quality
    Standards and WHO guideline levels
To inform considerations about the implications of the various emission scenarios related to exceedances
above EU air quality standards and WHO air quality guideline levels, background concentration values of
PM2.5 and NO2 have been computed with the GAINS model at a 7 km*7 km spatial resolution. (Calculations
for PM10 could not be performed for this report as these would require substantial methodological
updates in the GAINS model8). Also, in contrast to earlier studies, this analysis refrains from estimating
concentrations for individual monitoring sites, in particular since the GAINS methodology applied for this
report has not yet been upgraded to incorporate recent progress in fine-scale scale modelling of these
pollutants.
For exceedance analysis, background concentrations computed for the 7 km*7 km grid cells have been
allocated to the air quality management zones, and for each zone the maximum computed grid value has
been taken.

3.3 Health impact indicators
The GAINS model estimates health impact indicators along the health impact assessment methodology
that has been recommended for Europe by the World Health Organization (WHO) in its HRAPIE (Health
risks from air pollution in Europe) project (WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2013).
3.3.1 Indicators for exposure to PM2.5
With a specific focus on the epidemiological evidence gained in Europe and comparable regions, the
HRAPIE (Health risks from air pollution in Europe) project of the WHO recommended to estimate health
risks from long-term exposure to PM2.5:
       for all-cause mortality,
       considering only population above 30 years age,
       employing long-term (annual mean) PM2.5 levels that are typical for the exposure of a given
        population (e.g., PM2.5 levels measured at urban background stations, but not at hot spots),
       apply a linear exposure-response function to all PM2.5 caused by anthropogenic sources, and
       exclude exposure from natural sources of PM2.5 (e.g., soil dust) in the assessment.

Depending on the focus of an assessment, other studies apply different methods that can deliver
different results. For instance, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) project, with its global focus, estimates
specific health outcomes (e.g., lung cancer, acute lower respiratory infections, cardiovascular mortality,
cardiopulmonary mortality, etc.), which, however, do not capture all diseases for which exposure to
ambient air pollution is considered as a risk factor. To facilitate plausible results for rather high exposure
levels (above 100 µg/m3) that are often found in developing countries, GBD employs non-linear
‘Integrated Exposure Response’ functions to various health outcomes that deliver lower responses at
high exposure levels. However, the HRAPIE project found that for concentrations in the range where
epidemiological evidence exists (i.e., up to ~30 µg/m 3 PM2.5) and which are typical for Europe, linear
associations between exposure and response deliver a better statistical fit. Also, the GBD assessments
apply a general lower threshold of 3-7 µg/m3 to total PM2.5 including the fraction originating from natural
sources. As a consequence, GBD assessments suggest lower numbers than those computed with the
WHO/HRAPIE method used in this report.
In contrast, the figures calculated by GAINS are somewhat lower than the analyses of the European
Environment Agency (EEA). This is mainly due to the way in which exposure to PM2.5 from natural sources
is taken into account (this cannot be readily quantified from the available air quality monitoring data
unless the chemical composition of PM2.5 is analysed as well) and other methodological differences,
including the geographical scale of the analysis.
Against this background, the GAINS analysis computes the following indicators for health impacts from
PM2.5:
     Mean population exposure to PM2.5 in each Member State, computed at a 7 km*7 km spatial
      resolution.
     Annual cases of premature deaths attributable to exposure to PM 2.5. To preserve consistency with
      the calculations performed for the NEC Directive analyses which used constant 2010 population
      in order to disentangle the impacts of population development and environmental policies, these
      calculations are performed also for the future with the 2010 population figures. Thereby, future
      demographic changes do not affect the distribution of mitigation efforts among Member States.
      However, the use of constant 2010 figures introduces inaccuracies in the assessment of future
                                                     24
        health benefits, which scale with the change in total population. Thus, the accompanying
        assessment of the economic and health benefits from the CAO2 policy scenarios employs future
        population data that are projected for the different Member States.
     Alternatively, GAINS also provides the loss in statistical life expectancy that is attributable to a
      given exposure to PM2.5. This refers to the population older than 30 years in 2010 considering
      their remaining lifetime over the next 100 years. While this has been found by the HRAPIE project
      as the most meaningful metric, experience showed that it was difficult to communicate and hard
      to understand for the general public and decision makers.
     GAINS also provides the Years of Life Lost (YOLLs) attributable to the exposure to a given level of
      PM2.5, in terms of the cumulative number of life years lost that occur over the remaining life time
      of the population over 30 years living in 2010. Again, this excludes population dynamics, and is
      notably different from the annual YOLL estimates (in a particular year), which are most relevant
      for an economic assessment of the (annual) benefits of pollution control measures. Estimates of
      the annual YOLLs that take into account the population dynamics in the various Member States
      are provided in the accompanying benefits assessment report.

The evolution of the standard indicators that have been used in the analyses for the NEC Directive and
the Second Clean Air Outlook for ecosystems impacts for the various policy scenarios are shown in Table
3.11 to Table 3.14, and the potential changes from further climate and air policies and measures in Figure
3.13 to Figure 3.16. The assessment is fully compatible with the first Clean Air Outlook, as it employs the
same critical loads data, i.e., those approved by the Executive Body of the LRTAP Convention in fall 2017
for use in policy assessments. Critical loads (CLs) are steady-state deposition thresholds and are derived
for the impact of nitrogen (N) in terms of eutrophication and the combined impact of sulphur (S) and N
deposition on the acidity status of an ecosystem; and their value depends on the properties of the that
ecosystem. A CL is the maximum deposition rate of N (and S) compounds that do not lead to negative
impacts in the long run. If deposition(s) are higher than the CL of an ecosystem, the CL is said to be
exceeded (see Hettelingh et al., 2017 for more details).

        area). Only terrestrial ecosystems are included in the analysis.
       The total ecosystem area in the EU-27 where nitrogen deposition exceeds the critical loads for
        eutrophication (in km2 and as percentage of the total Natura2000 area).
       Forest area in the EU-27 where acid deposition exceeds the critical loads for acidification (in km 2
        and as percentage of the total forest area in the EU-27).
       The mean excess flux of ozone for deciduous trees in the EU-27. This is computed based on the

        developed under the Convention on Long-range Transboundary Air Pollution. The mean excess

3.5 Economic valuation and economic analysis
3.5.1 Valuation methods
The methods for the assessment of health impacts and associated values are reviewed in Annexes 1.3
and 1.4, and conclusions have been integrated to the ALPHA-Riskpoll (ARP) model (Holland et al, 2013)
which has been extensively used alongside GAINS in earlier cost-benefit analysis (CBA) of EU policies on
air quality (e.g., Amann et al, 2017). The Annexes review practice in health impact assessment and CBA
by international organisations including the EU, OECD, WHO and USEPA, and by analysts in various
European countries.
The recommendations of the HRAPIE (Health Response to Air Pollutants In Europe) study led by WHO-
Europe in 2013 (WHO, 2013) remain the most recent comprehensive review of air pollution epidemiology
in Europe. Since HRAPIE was completed, however, there has been a substantial expansion of literature
in the field and it became appropriate to consider updating the earlier methods with respect to effects
and response functions previously included in the analysis, and with respect to the addition of further
impacts. Annex 1.3 concludes that there are good grounds for considering that the HRAPIE
recommendations underestimate the health impacts of air pollution. Several recent studies reviewed in
Annex 1.3 consider additional impacts based on recent epidemiological literature (for example on
coronary heart disease, stroke, asthma and diabetes) that would add significantly to the benefits from
cleaner air quantified under HRAPIE. The WHO Systematic Review for PM 2.5 (Chen and Hoek, 2020), and
Pope et al (2020) also indicate that the HRAPIE function for mortality effects of long-term exposure to
PM2.5 is conservative. This is particularly important as the effect of PM 2.5 on mortality is dominant across
CBA studies in this field. There is, however, no consensus across the studies reviewed on which additional
effects should be included or, for mortality, which precise response function should be adopted. In the
context of the present analysis, the following positions are adopted:
•     The HRAPIE function set remains in use for the core estimates. This is acknowledged as a
      conservative position, biased to underestimation of health impacts. This position also enables
      comparison with earlier reports.
•     Supplementary analysis is used to provide an indication of possible levels of underestimation of
      impacts by using the HRAPIE functions. The supplementary analysis applied here includes:
           o An increased estimate of PM2.5 related mortality, using the relative risk of 1.08 per
               10 µg/m3 overall estimate from Chen and Hoek (2020), compared to 1.06 per 10 µg/m 3
               from HRAPIE. This is applied in the supplementary analysis rather than core because of
               variability in estimates from the Chen and Hoek (2020) and Pope et al (2020) studies. The
               overall estimate from Chen and Hoek is preferred to their estimate based on European
               studies only, given the much larger number of studies included in the former.
           o Additional response functions for stroke and cardiovascular disease via incidence of non-
               fatal myocardial infarction linked to PM2.5 exposure.

Inclusion of additional response functions for childhood asthma and diabetes was considered, though
these effects were only included in one source (Ricardo, 2019). They have not been quantified here. The
list of impacts considered in the health impact assessment is shown in Table 3.16.

Some health impacts are modelled by both GAINS and the ARP model, though results are not directly
comparable. The optimisation processes in GAINS account for change in risk, characterised through
reference to a static population, which is appropriate for the needs of cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA).
Including population change in GAINS would mean that differences in risk would be harder to interpret,
being partly due to differences in population and partly to differences in pollution. ARP, in contrast,
varies future population according to projections from World Population Prospects 9 to account for
changes in population size and age profile in order to be able to compare costs and benefits consistently
(bearing in mind that underlying demand projections for polluting services such as energy and transport
account for population change). There is a further difference in the way that mortality linked to chronic
exposure to PM2.5 is reported. GAINS estimates life years lost under steady state conditions over the
lifetime of the population (again, a risk-based approach appropriate to CEA), whilst ARP quantifies life
years lost from one year’s exposure in order to generate benefit estimates that can be compared with
annual pollution control costs.

3.5.3 Reduced damages to materials, crops, forests and ecosystems
Damage to building materials was extensively researched in the 1980s and 1990s. Although research in
the area continues, it is less active now than previously. Damage values per unit emission for SO 2 and
NOx have been taken from the CASES study (CASES, 2008).
The analysis of pollution damage to crops considers all agricultural crop production in the EU, focused on
the effects of ozone. Methods are taken from the ECLAIRE study (Holland et al, 2015), drawing
particularly on evidence generated through the Working Group on Effects under the Convention on Long

                                                      42
Range Transboundary Air Pollution. The tool developed in ECLAIRE has been updated for this analysis to
use agricultural production in 2018 for the baseline. This is held constant through the period of
assessment, though it is acknowledged that production patterns across Europe may change in the period
to 2050 as a consequence of climate change and other changes. No account is taken of impacts that may
affect production of animals and associated products (milk, eggs, honey, wool). The countries most
affected by crop damages are France, Italy and Spain, and the most affected crops are wheat, olives,
maize, grapes, rapeseed and tomato.
The analysis of forest damage takes account of ozone impacts leading to reduced productivity of wood
for sale and of reduced carbon sequestration. Methods are again taken from the ECLAIRE study (Holland
et al, 2015). Changes in productivity are valued against market data. Changes in carbon sequestration
are valued using recommendations from DG Mobility and Transport’s Handbook on the External Cost of
Transport (EC, 2019). These recommendations are to apply marginal carbon mitigation costs of €100/t
CO2 up to 2030 and €269/tonne thereafter (2016 prices). Low and high variants were applied in the
quantification of forest damage, with the Low estimate based on the application of carbon mitigation
costs of €100/t CO2 throughout, and the High estimate applying carbon mitigation costs of €269/t CO 2
from 2030 onwards.
Economic analysis of ecosystem damage is also based on the ECLAIRE study. It assesses impacts related
to terrestrial ecosystems only. ECLAIRE used three methods to estimate damage costs for ecosystems, a
willingness to pay (WTP) approach based on the results of research by Christie et al (2006, 2011, 2012),
a repair cost approach described by Ott et al (Ott et al, 2006) and a ‘regulatory revealed preference’
approach (developed in the ECLAIRE study). The focus of analysis is on exceedance of the critical load for
nitrogen in Natura 2000 sites, with valuation applied to the area subject to critical loads exceedance. No
account was taken of exceedance of the critical load for acidification, because the area concerned is far
less than that affected by eutrophication and there is potential for double counting if results for both
effects are combined. The WTP based approach is adopted here as the preferred option because it
follows a willingness to pay approach to valuation consistent with that used for other impacts assessed
in this report. Uncertainties in the methods used in ECLAIRE led to the use of a factor 3 variation between
low and high estimates of damage.

3.5.5 Macro-economic impacts
This section integrates the air pollution control costs and the corresponding benefits of clean air into the
broader, economy-wide framework as captured by the JRC-GEM-E3 model. Accounting for interactions
in the context of the full economic system is a valuable step in the analysis for several reasons. First, while
abatement expenditures are a cost for one sector, these expenditures also represent increased demand
for goods and services of the sectors that deliver the goods required in the abatement process. Second,
both the costs and the increased demand for abatement goods may have impacts along the supply chain.
These effects are included here, as the model builds on input-output tables of GTAP10 (Aguiar et al.,
2019). Third, costs and benefits for an economic sector may be exacerbated or attenuated by
international trade. Trade effects can amplify losses when a sector loses competitive advantage
compared to foreign firms. Alternatively, trade can limit losses when a sector has the possibility to
substitute expensive domestic inputs for imported ones. Fourth, general equilibrium feedbacks in
relation to the labour market can influence the economic outcome. For instance, cleaner air leads to a
reduction in sick leaves. When the implied increase in labour productivity translates into higher wages,
households experience higher purchasing power and may raise overall consumption levels. Finally, the
integrated economic perspective enables the analysis of economic effects at sector-specific level,
providing more insight into how efforts and gains are shared between different actors in the economy.
To assess the macro-economic impacts of the pollution control policies that are analysed in the scenarios,
country- and sector-specific abatement costs from the GAINS model are fed into the economy-wide JRC-
GEM-E3 model (Figure 3.17). GEM-E3 is a Computable General Equilibrium (CGE) model, representing
firms’ production choices, households’ consumption behaviour, international trade flows and
government policy and budgetary constraints. Abatement costs are represented as additional
intermediate inputs required in the production process. On the benefit side, two ‘market’ benefits,
calculated in the Alpha-RiskPoll framework, serve as input into JRC-GEM-E3: crop yield impacts are
translated into changes in total factor productivity of the crop sector, and changes in lost work days are
represented as a corresponding change in labour productivity. The framework also includes a direct link
from the Alpha-RiskPoll model to the economy-wide cost-benefit assessment, representing the non-
market benefits that are valued outside of the economic modelling, as presented in the previous sections.
This methodology follows the approach of the First Clean Air Outlook10. The framework is furthermore

approaches to include this evidence in the analysis are identified: The first approach is based on the
estimate that output per worker increases by 0.80% per 1 µgm -3 decrease in the concentration of fine
particulate matter. This is consistent with recent findings for the impact of PM 2.5 on productivity in the
manufacturing sector in the US (Chang et al., 2016; 0.8% per µgm -3) and slightly higher than recent
estimates for the service sector productivity in China (Kahn and Li, 2020; 0.35% per µgm -3). We interpret
this estimate as a response in labour productivity, while assuming a linear exposure-response function
such that this point estimate applies across scenarios and regions, also for future years. The second
approach builds on the estimate that GDP increases by 0.83% per 1 µgm -3 decrease in the concentration
of PM2.5. To translate this estimate into an input shock for the JRC-GEM-E3 model, we maintain
consistency with the labour productivity shock of 0.80% per µgm-3 and complement this with an equal
productivity shock for capital. As the econometric study of Dechezleprêtre et al. (2019) cannot pin down
exactly the channels through which air pollution affects aggregate economic output, we have chosen
here to use a shock to total factor productivity – labour and capital (including land) – as this has the
advantage to reflect the empirical evidence of output per worker while replicating aggregate GDP impacts
at the same time. Prior to running the scenarios based on this second approach, we calibrate the JRC-
GEM-E3 model to the macro-economic estimates, using a small and uniform (across regions and capital
and labour productivity) scaling factor to replicate the 0.83% of GDP per 1 µgm-3 exactly, after general
equilibrium interactions. The corresponding productivity shocks are then combined with the changes in
PM2.5 concentrations. As the analysis is done at the Member State level, we do not study how region-
specific changes in air pollution may coincide with the particular location of industries within a country.
We furthermore abstract from potential productivity impact differences across sectors, although this
could be included in future work if the empirical evidence is sufficiently strong.
Figure 3.17 illustrates that the magnitude of these estimates influences the cost-benefit assessment,
which now yields positive GDP impacts across all scenarios. This is true even when only considering the
labour productivity impact, which implicitly covers both the extensive (e.g., reduced sick leave and
absence from work) and the intensive (on-the-job performance) margin of labour productivity. In the
best case (1.5 LIFE+WAM), GDP increases by 1.3% compared to the baseline in 2050. In all climate policy
scenarios, economic benefits linked to cleaner air thanks to climate and clean air policies offset all the air
pollution control costs, plus a substantial share of climate policy costs. Previous estimates with the JRC-
GEM-E3 model for GDP impacts of climate policy in the 1.5 TECH scenario range from -0.29% to -1.30%
of GDP in 205013, such that the gains presented here (1.5 TECH+WAM) would offset from 20% to well
over 100% of these costs. Importantly, these are ‘market’ impacts in the sense that they translate into
GDP numbers, without explicitly considering the non-market value of avoided mortality. Accounting for
avoided mortality in a broader welfare metric, for instance through a willingness-to-pay approach, would
further raise the benefits over the GDP estimate shown here.

3.7.3 Air quality benefits of emission controls on other countries
The import-export matrices can also be used to reveal the extent to which the benefits from emission
control measures in a country occur within the same country, and how much of the total benefits
materialise outside. This is of particular importance for national benefits analyses of domestic air
pollution control policies and measures, which often ignore the benefits outside their own country and
thereby provide only an incomplete account of benefits.

reductions within the same country and in the other EU Member States (Figure 3.21). The calculation
takes into account the changes in precursor emissions between 2015 and 2030 baseline projection, the
dispersion and transport of the PM2.5 precursor emissions in the atmosphere, how this influences
ambient PM2.5 levels across the EU, and how many people are affected by these changes (using

4 Key findings
This report presents an outlook on the likely evolution of air quality in the European Union and on
associated health, ecosystems and economic impacts. To inform the development of the Second Clean
Air Outlook, the model analysis has incorporated the most recent information provided by Member
States on national emission inventories as well as on additional air pollution and climate policies and
measures that are already selected for implementation. Also, the latest projections of energy and
agricultural activities have been included. Health impact assessment methods and approaches for the
economic valuation of benefits have been updated based on reviews of the latest scientific literature,
indicating larger benefits from clean air than estimated previously.
The updated model framework clearly shows that, with full implementation of all currently existing
sectoral legislation regulating air pollution and of the measures needed to reach the Climate and Energy
targets for 2030 that were set in 2018, the NEC Directive requirements would be fulfilled at the EU level
in 2030 for all pollutants except ammonia. However, this hides differences among Member States and in
some cases even NAPCP measures would not be sufficient to reach the NEC Directive emission reduction
commitments (especially in the case of ammonia).
In the same context, in all air quality management zones, background concentrations of PM 2.5 would fall
below the current limit value of 25 µg /m3. In 90 percent of all air quality management zones, background
concentrations of PM2.5 would decline below the current WHO guidelines value of 10 µg/m³ (in 2015,
background PM2.5 levels were below the guideline value in only 41% of the air quality management
zones). Full implementation of all technically feasible air pollution control measures combined with
further climate policies could bring background concentrations of PM2.5 below the WHO guideline in
almost 99% of the zones.
The parallel decline in population exposure to PM2.5 will be mirrored by significantly lower health impacts
by 2030. If all policies that Member States have already agreed to are fully implemented, it is estimated
that the envisaged air quality improvements will save annually more than 210,000 cases of premature
deaths attributable to PM2.5, which accounts for about 55% of the number in 2005.
When taking into account the additional policies and measures reported by Member States in their
NAPCP (only the ones which were defined with enough detail to be quantified though), around 7,000
additional premature deaths due to PM2.5 alone would be avoided annually in the EU, for each year
between 2030 to 2050. These NAPCP measures would bring health benefits between EUR 8-43 billion per
year (the range represents the variety of valuation assumptions) and always bring a net benefit to society.
Full implementation of all technically feasible emission controls could save another 30,000 lives every
year. However, this would still leave more than 130,000 premature deaths in the EU due to PM 2.5 pollution
alone, mostly due to pollution coming from outside of the EU including international maritime activities.
In addition to their health benefits, the envisaged emission reductions will also enhance the sustainability
of ecosystems. However, excess nitrogen deposition, an important risk factor for biodiversity of
terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems, will remain of high concern, as the critical loads for eutrophication
will remain widely exceeded. The emission reduction requirements of the NEC Directive include NH3
emissions, as an important precursor for eutrophication. Still, a large potential for further reduction
through changes in agricultural practices and lifestyle changes (including human diets) will still remain.
Such additional reductions could not only make important contributions towards the protection of
biodiversity of the European ecosystems, especially in the Natura 2000 nature protection areas, but at
the same time improve human health through lower PM2.5 levels and reduce emissions of greenhouse
gases.

                                                    68
The model analyses conducted for this report clearly indicate that additional actions towards cleaner air
always bring net benefits to society. Also, in general, climate policy measures deliver co-benefits on air
quality, although an enhanced use of fuel wood in the residential sector can counteract clean air efforts
through higher emissions of PM2.5.
Due to the residence time of PM2.5 in the atmosphere air of several days, PM2.5 can be transported over
several hundreds of kilometres. As a consequence, PM2.5 at any given location originates from sources in
a large area, often in other countries. The high share of PM 2.5 background concentration originating from
other Member States and non-EU countries underlines the need for EU legislation and for the EU to take
international action. In the past, such coordinated EU-wide action has shown the increased reductions in
background PM2.5 concentrations compared to the impacts of domestic actions in the same country.
Increasing marginal abatement costs call for even further EU-wide and international coordination, in
order to realise the potentials for additional air quality improvements at least cost.
In the long run, with the further policies and measures that have been analysed in this report, it seems
possible to bring background concentrations of PM2.5 below the current WHO guideline value of 10 µg/m3
throughout Europe. Possible exceptions remain at local hot spots that are not captured by the spatial
resolution of this analysis.
